Aluminum Smelting Sensors

inductive sensing resonant capacitive COMSOL molten cryolite IEEE published

// the problem

Aluminum smelting cells are one of the harshest measurement environments on Earth. The electrolytic reduction process runs at roughly 960 °C, submerged in molten cryolite — a highly corrosive fluoride salt — while drawing tens of thousands of amperes of DC current. Inside each cell, a layer of molten cryolite (the bath) sits above a pool of molten aluminum metal. Precise control of the bath thickness and the aluminum fluoride (AlF₃) concentration in that bath are critical to process efficiency and product quality.

Despite this, the industry's standard measurement method is still a technician manually inserting a dipstick into the pot. Real-time, in-situ sensing has remained an open problem — every contact sensor corrodes or fails quickly, and the intense electromagnetic environment rules out most non-contact approaches. These two projects address that gap with two independently developed sensor principles.

// sensor 1 — inductive level measurement

The first sensor targets where the materials are: bath thickness and molten aluminum depth. The approach uses eddy-current induction — a coil placed above the cell induces currents in the conductive molten layers, and the resulting impedance change encodes depth information without any contact with the melt.

Two configurations were developed: a single-inductor design that establishes the core measurement principle, and a differential induction-balance configuration that significantly improves resolution by cancelling common-mode interference. Both were validated analytically, through COMSOL electromagnetic simulation, and in furnace experiments with real molten cryolite — demonstrating clear discrimination between bath and metal layer depths.

// sensor 2 — resonant capacitive composition measurement

The second sensor targets what the bath is made of: specifically, AlF₃ concentration. Fluoride concentration drifts during smelting and must be held in a tight window to control liquidus temperature and bath conductivity — but measuring it in real time has been an unsolved problem.

The solution is a ceramic-coated passive LC resonator probe that partially submerges in the bath. As bath conductivity changes with AlF₃ concentration, the quality factor of the resonator detunes in a predictable, measurable way. Reading out concentration becomes an impedance measurement — robust, contact-tolerant through the ceramic coating, and validated across multiple AlF₃ concentrations in molten cryolite.

This work was presented at the IEEE Sensors Conference and awarded Outstanding Lecture among over 400 accepted contributions.

// field validation

Both sensors were validated in furnace experiments using real molten cryolite — not synthetic substitutes. The inductive sensor demonstrated repeatable depth discrimination across bath and metal layers. The resonant capacitive sensor produced consistent impedance readings across varying AlF₃ concentrations. These results establish a concrete path toward closing the loop on continuous, automated bath geometry and composition control in industrial smelting cells.
